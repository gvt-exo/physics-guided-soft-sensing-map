\begin{abstract}
Reliable soft sensing is needed when industrial quality measurements are sparse and operating conditions change after model calibration. This study evaluates the retrospective transfer performance of a physics-guided soft sensor for molar ratio and downstream solution density in industrial monoammonium phosphate production. The analysis uses 260,642 one-minute process records and 1,325 paired laboratory measurements. A gray-box model reconstructs latent phosphoric-acid properties from a preprocessed conductivity signal and propagates them through a material balance. It is compared with ridge regression, gradient boosting, and a physics-only reference using chronological validation: nine within-regime rolling tests, a regime-boundary test, an independent post-gap evaluation on 438 future laboratory pairs, and ten post-gap rolling comparisons. Conductivity inputs to the fitted latent-property mapping are constrained to the corresponding training range to prevent uncontrolled extrapolation. In the independent future-period test, ridge regression achieved root-mean-square errors of 0.0286 for molar ratio and 0.0141~g~cm$^{-3}$ for density, compared with 0.1795 and 0.0456~g~cm$^{-3}$ for the frozen physics-guided model. Gradient boosting achieved a density error of 0.0128~g~cm$^{-3}$. Rolling recalibration improved the physics-guided estimates but did not restore competitive molar-ratio accuracy. The results show why training-domain checks, target-specific performance monitoring, recalibration, and fallback rules are needed before relying on soft-sensor estimates in plant operations. Because some signal-processing steps are noncausal, the reported performance is retrospective rather than a demonstration of online closed-loop control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reliable measurement of process quality variables is essential for monitoring, supervision, and control, yet industrial laboratories often provide such measurements only at sparse and irregular intervals. Soft sensors use continuously available process measurements to estimate variables that cannot be measured directly or economically at the required frequency \citep{kadlec2009,souza2016}. An estimator intended to support plant decisions must maintain acceptable performance beyond the data on which it was calibrated, not merely reproduce historical observations.

In industrial practice, changes in operating conditions, process instrumentation, or the relationship between measured and unmeasured states can invalidate a soft-sensor calibration. Missing historian data, asynchronous laboratory sampling, sensor noise, and gaps in the recorded time series complicate both model development and assessment \citep{kadlec2009,offermans2024,dai2025}. The control-engineering literature has addressed these challenges through adaptive and transfer-learning soft sensors. For example, moving-window supervised latent-factor analysis has been proposed for state-shifting processes \citep{yao2017}, an online transfer-kernel algorithm has been studied for variable working conditions \citep{zhang2023cep}, and automated soft-sensor design has been demonstrated for industrial applications \citep{cao2025cep}. These studies motivate practical model updating but also underline the importance of testing whether a fixed model remains reliable when operating conditions change.

Physics-guided and hybrid soft sensors offer an alternative to unconstrained statistical regression by incorporating known process relationships, such as material balances, into an empirical estimator \citep{sansana2021,bradley2022,schweidtmann2024,mousa2025}. Such structure can make predictions easier to interpret; however, it does not guarantee reliable transfer if the empirical relationships used to estimate latent process properties are no longer valid. This is a particularly important distinction in applications where measurements of those latent properties are unavailable during routine operation.

The industrial case considered here is monoammonium phosphate (MAP) production. Product-related molar ratio and downstream solution density are available from sparse laboratory or operator records, while process telemetry is sampled approximately once per minute. A conductivity signal acts as a proxy for unmeasured phosphoric-acid properties. Fertilizer-production soft sensing has been investigated previously \citep{fu2007}; the present work is not intended to claim a new general soft-sensor architecture or a new control algorithm. Instead, it provides an industrial validation study of what happens when a physically structured estimator is transferred across a change in process conditions, including a long gap in available data.

The central question is whether physically informed estimation remains sufficiently accurate for monitoring when its data-fitted latent-property relationships are carried beyond their training period. We address it by (i) evaluating a gray-box model against ridge regression, gradient boosting, and a physics-only reference on the same laboratory observations; (ii) separating the effect of a training-range extrapolation guard from temporal model transfer; (iii) evaluating an independent future period in addition to shorter chronological windows; and (iv) comparing frozen and recalibrated estimators under identical post-gap test conditions. The aim is to derive evidence-based recommendations for model validation and maintenance in a plant-monitoring setting. Online deployment and closed-loop control are not evaluated.

\section{Industrial process and data}

\subsection{Process description}

The investigated process is part of an industrial MAP production line. Clarified phosphoric acid is transferred through an intermediate stage to a tubular neutralization reactor. Ammonia is introduced for neutralization and process water is adjusted to influence downstream solution density. The principal reaction is represented in simplified form as
\begin{equation}
NH_3 + H_3PO_4 \rightarrow NH_4H_2PO_4.
\end{equation}

The two quality variables considered in this work are the $NH_3/H_3PO_4$ molar ratio $R$ and downstream solution density $\rho_M$. The nominal operating targets considered in the study were
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{target}}=1.06,
\qquad
\rho_{M,\mathrm{target}}=1.21\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
\end{equation}

The reference values were obtained from sparse laboratory/operator measurements rather than at the one-minute frequency of the process historian. The average interval between laboratory measurements was approximately 4~h. Two phosphoric-acid properties needed by the material-balance calculation, the $P_2O_5$ fraction and acid density, were not continuously measured with sufficient reliability and were therefore treated as latent variables.

\subsection{Data quality and preprocessing}

The source data contain noisy process signals, short spikes and dropouts, missing or invalid values, incomplete time-series segments, and operating-state changes. 

The preprocessing workflow is:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
\item read the native one-minute process series;
\item sort channels jointly by timestamp and preserve large acquisition gaps;
\item select the active redundant flow channel where applicable;
\item apply process-specific validity rules to exclude invalid and non-production measurements;
\item apply conductivity validity filtering and flow despiking;
\item apply process plausibility and range checks;
\item construct timestamps at which all required inputs are jointly valid;
\item associate each laboratory observation with an eligible process window determined by lag $\tau$ and window length $W$.
\end{enumerate}

Process-specific validity rules were applied to the telemetry channels to remove invalid measurements, short abnormal excursions, and observations outside the production domain before smoothing, model fitting, and laboratory-window aggregation. The same preprocessing rules were applied consistently across the analyzed periods and did not use laboratory target values. Because the one-month data gap exceeds the continuity limit, temporal filters reset at gaps longer than 5 minutes; the gap-aware processing therefore produces two independent telemetry segments.

The present analysis is retrospective. The evaluated signal-processing pipeline includes centered robust filtering, Savitzky--Golay smoothing, and piecewise cubic Hermite interpolating polynomial (PCHIP) reconstruction; these operations can use neighboring process values on both sides of a timestamp. Laboratory alignment likewise uses a centered process window around $t_{\mathrm{lab}}-\tau$, so when $\tau<W/2$ part of that window can occur after the nominal laboratory timestamp. No outer-test laboratory targets are used in preprocessing, fitting, or hyperparameter selection. Nevertheless, because the process-signal preprocessing is noncausal, the reported errors should be interpreted as retrospective model-transfer performance rather than end-to-end prospective online performance. A real-time implementation would require causal filtering and one-sided aggregation, followed by separate prospective validation.

\subsection{Physics-guided soft sensor}

The physics-guided model is a gray-box sequence rather than a direct unconstrained input--output regression. The latent acid state is represented by the $P_2O_5$ content $w_{P_2O_5}$ (mass \%) and phosphoric-acid density $\rho_A$ (g~cm$^{-3}$). Before evaluating the fitted conductivity-to-composition mapping, the conductivity input is restricted to the support observed in the corresponding training set,
\begin{equation}
\widetilde c=
\min\!\left[
\max\!\left(c,c_{\min}^{\mathrm{train}}\right),
c_{\max}^{\mathrm{train}}
\right].
\end{equation}
The bounds $c_{\min}^{\mathrm{train}}$ and $c_{\max}^{\mathrm{train}}$ are computed from training inputs only; no test targets are used. This guard prevents the polynomial or spline mapping from numerically extrapolating outside its fitted conductivity domain without removing difficult test observations.

The guarded conductivity is mapped to latent $P_2O_5$ content,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{w}_{P_2O_5}=f_{\theta}(\widetilde c),
\end{equation}
and the estimated composition is mapped to acid density,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\rho}_{A}=g_{\phi}\!\left(\widehat{w}_{P_2O_5}\right).
\end{equation}
The candidates for $f_{\theta}$ are linear, quadratic, and PCHIP mappings. The density mapping $g_{\phi}$ is quadratic.

Using $\widehat{\rho}_{A}$ in g~cm$^{-3}$ and $Q_A$ in m$^3$~h$^{-1}$, the acid-solution and $P_2O_5$ mass flows are
\begin{equation}
\dot m_A=1000\,\widehat{\rho}_A Q_A,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\dot m_{P_2O_5}=\dot m_A\frac{\widehat{w}_{P_2O_5}}{100}.
\end{equation}
The equivalent 100\% phosphoric-acid mass flow is
\begin{equation}
\dot m_{H_3PO_4,100\%}=1.38\,\dot m_{P_2O_5}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding molar flows are
\begin{equation}
\dot n_{H_3PO_4}=
\frac{\dot m_{H_3PO_4,100\%}}{M_{H_3PO_4}},
\qquad
\dot n_{NH_3}=
\frac{\dot m_N}{M_{NH_3}},
\end{equation}
with $M_{H_3PO_4}=0.098$ kg~mol$^{-1}$ and $M_{NH_3}=0.017$ kg~mol$^{-1}$. The predicted ammonia-to-phosphoric-acid molar ratio is
\begin{equation}
\widehat R=
\frac{\dot n_{NH_3}}{\dot n_{H_3PO_4}}.
\end{equation}

The downstream-density calculation uses an additive-volume approximation for the acid solution, ammonia, and process-water streams,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\rho}_M=
\frac{1}{1000}
\frac{
\dot m_A+\dot m_N+\rho_W Q_W
}{
Q_A+\dot m_N/\rho_N+Q_W
},
\end{equation}
where $\rho_W=998.23$ kg~m$^{-3}$ and $\rho_N=680$ kg~m$^{-3}$. The factor $1/1000$ converts the resulting density from kg~m$^{-3}$ to g~cm$^{-3}$.

\subsection{Parameter estimation and physical regularization}

The latent-property mappings are not fitted to continuously measured $P_2O_5$ or acid-density labels, because such measurements are not available at process frequency. Instead, their parameters are estimated jointly from the sparse laboratory molar-ratio and downstream-density references in each training set. Diagnostic values of acid density and $P_2O_5$ are back-calculated from the laboratory targets and concurrent flows only to initialize the optimization; they are not treated as independent supervised targets.

For a training set, residuals in molar ratio and downstream density are standardized by their respective training-set standard deviations and combined using a Huber loss with threshold $\delta=1.5$. The objective also contains weak $L_2$ regularization on the mapping parameters ($10^{-4}$), a second-difference smoothness penalty for PCHIP knots ($10^{-2}$), a soft acid-density prior ($10^{-2}$), and a monotonicity penalty ($10^{-2}$) that discourages decreasing acid density with increasing $P_2O_5$. The density prior used in the penalty is
\begin{equation}
\rho_A^{\mathrm{prior}}(w)=0.00008w^2+0.0063w+0.9977,
\end{equation}
with $w$ in mass \% and $\rho_A^{\mathrm{prior}}$ in g~cm$^{-3}$. The acid-density prior residual is scaled by 0.10~g~cm$^{-3}$, and the monotonicity penalty uses a derivative scale of 0.01~g~cm$^{-3}$ per mass-\% $P_2O_5$. Parameters are optimized by Nelder--Mead search with at most 3000 iterations and 12,000 function evaluations. These regularization terms constrain the fitted latent states but do not use test-period target values.

\subsection{Time alignment and model selection}

Candidate lags and aggregation windows are
\begin{equation}
\tau\in\{0,10,\ldots,180\}\ \mathrm{min},
\qquad
W\in\{10,20,30,60\}\ \mathrm{min}.
\end{equation}

For each validation scenario, lag, window, and physics-guided mapping type are selected using only the corresponding training data. The first 9 days of a training interval are used for inner fitting and the following 5 days for inner chronological validation. The selection score is based on root-mean-square error (RMSE):
\begin{equation}
J=
\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_R}{0.03}
+
\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_{\rho}}{0.01}.
\end{equation}

The selected configuration is then frozen for the relevant outer scenario, while fitted coefficients are re-estimated from each outer training interval only. The selected physics-guided configurations are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item pre-gap within-regime validation: $\tau=90$ min, $W=60$ min, PCHIP spline;
\item A-to-B transfer: $\tau=100$ min, $W=30$ min, quadratic mapping;
\item strict external frozen model: $\tau=0$ min, $W=60$ min, quadratic mapping;
\item post-gap adapted model: $\tau=140$ min, $W=10$ min, PCHIP spline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baseline models}

All baseline models use exactly the same temporally aligned rows and the same four process features as the physics-guided model: conductivity, acid flow, ammonia flow, and water flow. Separate target models are fitted for molar ratio and downstream density where applicable.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Ridge regression}: the four inputs are standardized from the training set. The regularization parameter is selected from $\{0,\allowbreak 10^{-4},\allowbreak 10^{-3},\allowbreak 10^{-2},\allowbreak 10^{-1},\allowbreak 1,\allowbreak 10,\allowbreak 100\}$ using an inner chronological split in which the earlier approximately two thirds of the outer training period are used for fitting and the final third for validation. The selected model is then refitted on the full outer training set.
\item \textbf{Physics-only reference}: the same material balance is evaluated with $P_2O_5$ fixed at 52~wt\% and acid density given by the unfitted prior relation above. This model contains no target-fitted parameters and is intended as a mechanistic reference rather than a calibrated first-principles model.
\item \textbf{Gradient boosting}: least-squares boosting (LSBoost) uses 100 regression trees, learning rate 0.05, minimum leaf size 10, and at most 20 splits per tree. These hyperparameters are fixed for all outer splits; separate ensembles are fitted for the two targets.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chronological validation design}

Validation is entirely temporal. No random shuffling is used.

\textbf{Pre-gap evaluation.} The prespecified outer protocol is a fixed 14-day training interval followed immediately by a 7-day held-out interval. Within Regime~A, rolling origins advance by 7 days and yield nine complete tests (A01--A09). Regime~B does not contain the 21 days of paired laboratory coverage required for a complete within-regime 14+7-day split.

\textbf{Short-term regime-transfer evaluation.} Cross-regime transfer uses 7 May--21 May 2026 for training and 21 May--28 May 2026 for testing.

\textbf{External post-gap evaluation.} The strict external test trains on all eligible pre-gap observations and evaluates on the post-gap period 7 July--29 September 2026. This scenario is denoted \texttt{external\_transfer\_full}.

\textbf{Post-gap frozen-versus-adapted comparison.} Ten post-gap calendar windows (P01--P10) are considered, each using 14 days for training and the next 7 days for testing. A window enters the pooled comparison only if temporal alignment leaves at least 40 training and 10 common test laboratory pairs. All ten windows satisfy this pre-specified criterion and therefore contribute to the pooled comparison. Rolling training intervals overlap, whereas the held-out test intervals do not; pooled metrics are therefore treated as descriptive summaries rather than independent-fold confidence estimates.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

For target $y_i$ and prediction $\hat y_i$, the mean absolute error (MAE) is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i-\hat y_i|,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Bias}=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat y_i-y_i).
\end{equation}

We also report the 95th percentile (P95) of the absolute error and the fractions within
\begin{equation}
|R-\widehat R|\le0.03,
\qquad
|\rho_M-\widehat{\rho}_M|\le0.01\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
\end{equation}

Molar-ratio predictions from the physics-guided implementation are rounded to two decimal places before validation, matching the production reporting setting.

The frozen physics-guided model shows a substantial negative molar-ratio bias and positive density bias over the future period and does not match the transfer performance of the statistical baselines.

The persistence of this performance gap over the full July--September period shows that the weak transfer of the physics-guided calibration is not confined to the short interval around the 21 May regime boundary. Although the downstream material-balance relations remain physically interpretable, the learned mappings used to reconstruct latent acid properties remain data-dependent; changes in these mappings can therefore propagate through an otherwise physically structured calculation.

\subsection{Discussion: temporal robustness and deployment implications}

The central result is that interpretability and predictive transfer are distinct properties. Figure~\ref{fig:postgaprmse} places the frozen and adapted errors on the same tolerance-normalized scale and makes the model-selection trade-off explicit: adaptation improves the physics-guided model, particularly for density, but the statistical baselines retain lower held-out errors. Across the chronological tests, Ridge regression and gradient boosting transfer more accurately than the physics-guided model, even though the latter embeds a chemically meaningful latent-state reconstruction and material balance. This finding is consistent with a broader process-systems perspective in which reliability depends not only on model structure but also on whether the relationships used by that structure remain valid as the process evolves \citep{kadlec2009,offermans2024,metcalfe2025,zhai2025}.

The external test identifies the transfer failure more clearly than the short A-to-B experiment. The material-balance equations themselves do not depend on the operating regime, but the conductivity-to-$P_2O_5$ and $P_2O_5$-to-density mappings are estimated from data. The observed systematic bias is therefore consistent with temporal change in one or both empirical latent-state relationships. Because these latent properties are not measured continuously, the present data do not identify which mapping changed or establish a causal mechanism for the shift. In this sense, the model is physics-guided but not demonstrably regime-invariant. The result cautions against treating the presence of first-principles equations as evidence of extrapolative validity without an explicit test of the data-dependent submodels.

The training-domain guard addresses a different problem. Restricting conductivity to the training support prevents uncontrolled polynomial or spline extrapolation and removes a source of numerical instability, but it cannot recover a changed latent-state relationship. Domain guarding should therefore be interpreted as a safety constraint, not as a substitute for transfer validation. This distinction is important for hybrid models because numerical plausibility can otherwise be mistaken for predictive reliability.

The target-dependent response to adaptation indicates that a single recalibration rule need not restore all outputs simultaneously. A practical soft-sensor lifecycle should therefore include distribution- or process-state monitoring, periodic comparison with new laboratory measurements, target-specific acceptance criteria, and a validated fallback predictor or an explicit unavailable/unreliable state when the current operating condition is outside the validated regime \citep{kay2024,metcalfe2025}.

Several boundaries of the present evidence are important. First, the signal processing and laboratory-window aggregation are retrospective and partly noncausal, as described in Section~2.3; the reported errors quantify temporal model transfer after retrospective preprocessing and are not direct estimates of closed-loop online performance. Second, the study concerns one industrial process and one extended observation period, so the numerical ranking of models should not be assumed to generalize to other plants or chemistries. Third, unmeasured process changes may contribute to the observed regime shift, and the externally observed 21 May boundary is used for evaluation rather than interpreted causally. Finally, the proprietary raw historian and laboratory records cannot be released, although the analysis code, validation workflow, and de-identified derived results are public. These limitations do not alter the within-study model comparison because all models are evaluated on identical chronological samples, but they define the scope of the conclusions.

\section{Conclusions}

An industrial soft sensor for monoammonium phosphate production was assessed using 260,642 minute-scale process observations and 1,325 paired laboratory measurements. The study compared a physics-guided latent-property estimator with ridge regression, gradient boosting, and a physics-only reference under chronological regime-transfer and recalibration experiments.

The training-range guard prevents unconstrained extrapolation of the empirical conductivity mapping but does not ensure accuracy when process conditions change. In the independent post-gap test on 438 future laboratory pairs, the frozen physics-guided model had root-mean-square errors of 0.1795 for molar ratio and 0.0456~g~cm$^{-3}$ for density, while ridge regression achieved 0.0286 and 0.0141~g~cm$^{-3}$, respectively. Gradient boosting achieved the lowest density error (0.0128~g~cm$^{-3}$). Across ten post-gap rolling windows with 357 held-out pairs, recalibration reduced the physics-guided model's errors, but its molar-ratio estimates remained less accurate than the statistical alternatives.

For industrial monitoring, physical interpretability and predictive reliability must be verified separately. The observed performance differences support chronological qualification tests, training-domain checks, monitoring against newly acquired laboratory measurements, target-specific recalibration criteria, and fallback rules for unreliable estimates. These are recommendations derived from retrospective validation, not claims of implemented online monitoring or control. Prospective deployment requires causal signal processing and separate live validation.
